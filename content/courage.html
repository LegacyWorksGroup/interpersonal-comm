Clear Communication, Courageous Conversations

Most of us can name a conversation we have been putting off. A partner who keeps missing deadlines. A colleague whose comment in a meeting landed badly. A client whose expectations have quietly drifted from what we agreed to. We tell ourselves we are waiting for the right moment, but often we are waiting for the discomfort to go away. It rarely does, and in the meantime, the problem tends to grow. Having that conversation takes courage: stepping into uncertainty with openness, speaking honestly and with compassion, and listening deeply to the other person’s experience.

This session is designed to strengthen your communication skills and build capacity to engage in candid, sometimes difficult conversations about sensitive topics or conflicts with clarity, empathy, and effectiveness. We will explore the fundamentals of how we communicate and unpack why courageous conversations are valuable. We will share practical tips and approaches and apply them to real-world scenarios through guided practice. Building these skills supports confidence, authenticity, and stronger collaborative relationships.

Why Courageous Conversations?

In the first session, we asked: What do I need, what do you need, and how do we agree to work together so everyone can do their best? Working agreements are a great start. But sooner or later, an agreement slips, a need goes unmet, or something lands badly. That’s when we need courageous conversations.

This session is organized around a single question:

How do I say what needs to be said, and hear what the other person needs me to hear, without making it harder than it has to be?

We will work on that question from the inside out:

  • The Ladder of Inference helps us notice what is happening in me: how quickly we move from what we saw to the story we are telling about it.
  • Nonviolent Communication gives us a way to say it clearly: to name what happened, how it affected us, what we need, and what we are asking for.
  • Listening for understanding helps us hear the other person: to find the needs underneath their words, which is often where common ground lives.

The Cost of Avoiding

Brené Brown popularized a simple phrase: “Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind.” It is tempting to believe that staying quiet, hinting, or softening a message until it disappears is the kind thing to do. Usually, it isn’t. When we avoid a hard conversation:

  • The other person loses the chance to understand the impact of their actions and to respond
  • We fill the silence with our own stories about what is going on, and those stories tend to get worse over time
  • Frustration leaks out sideways, in tone, in side conversations, or in withdrawal
  • Small, fixable problems become bigger, harder ones
  • Agreements stop meaning anything, because no one speaks up when agreements are broken

Courageous conversations are not about being blunt or winning an argument. They are about caring enough about the work and the relationship to say the true thing, with compassion. This is what we mean by candid communication: sharing truth while owning our impact on others, and when we are out of alignment, naming it and addressing it in real time. It is also an act of creative courage. Setting clear but kind boundaries is part of how we take care of ourselves, each other, and the work.

Fundamentals: Intent and Impact

Every message has two sides: what I meant (my intent) and how it landed (its impact). We judge ourselves by our intentions and others by their impact. When the two don’t match, each person tends to feel misunderstood: “That’s not what I meant!” and “But that’s how it came across!” are both true.

A few things widen the gap between intent and impact:

  • Channel. Text strips out tone of voice, facial expression, and timing. A short email that felt efficient to write can feel curt to read. The harder the message, the richer the channel it deserves (an in person conversation, call, or video rather than a chat message).
  • Power and position. The same words carry more weight coming from a supervisor, a funder, or a lead organization. Being aware of your position is part of communicating clearly.
  • Our own state. When we are tired, rushed, or anxious, a different part of us may be driving the bus, and our words come out differently than we intend.
  • Different thinking preferences. People listen for different things.

How Each Thinking Style Hears a Hard Message

Recall the Whole Brain Model from Session 1. When we are on the receiving end of a difficult conversation, our thinking preferences shape what we listen for and what helps us take it in.

Quadrant Thinking style Listens for What helps
A - Blue Analytical What exactly happened? Specific facts and examples, not generalizations
B - Green Practical What happens next? A clear request and concrete next steps
C - Red Relational Are we okay? Acknowledgement of the relationship and of feelings on both sides
D - Yellow Experimental Why does this matter? The bigger picture and room to think about new options

Our natural tendency is to deliver hard messages in the way we would want to hear them. Thinking about the other person’s preferences can help you decide what to lead with, and what not to leave out. Adapting our language, tone, and style to the person in front of us is how candid communication connects effectively across differences.

Noticing Our Own Reactions: The Ladder of Inference

Before we can communicate clearly with someone else, it helps to notice what is going on inside ourselves. The Ladder of Inference, developed by organizational psychologist Chris Argyris and popularized by Peter Senge and colleagues in The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook, describes how quickly, and mostly unconsciously, we move from something we observe to a conclusion and an action.

Picture a ladder. At the bottom is everything that actually happened. Each step up takes us further from it:

Rung What happens Example
7. Actions We act based on our beliefs I stop including them in early planning conversations
6. Beliefs We adopt beliefs about the world and this person “I can’t count on them”
5. Conclusions We draw conclusions “They aren’t committed to this collaborative”
4. Assumptions We make assumptions based on the meanings we added “If they cared, they’d have told us sooner”
3. Meaning We add meaning, based on our culture and experience “A one-line reply means they don’t take this seriously”
2. Selected data We notice some of it, and filter out the rest I notice how short their message was
1. Observable data What actually happened, as a camera would record it They posted: “Our section will be late.”

Two things make the ladder especially tricky:

  • We climb it in seconds. It usually doesn’t feel like we’re making assumptions at all. It feels like we are just seeing what is obviously true.
  • It’s a loop. Our beliefs shape what data we notice next time. Once I believe someone is unreliable, I’m more likely to notice every late reply and less likely to notice when they come through. This is called the reflexive loop.

None of this means our conclusions are always wrong. It means they are worth checking before we act on them, especially when the stakes are high.

The Story I’m Telling Myself

Brené Brown offers a simple phrase that brings the ladder into a conversation: “The story I’m telling myself is…” Brown notes that our brains reward us for creating a story, even when it’s incomplete. Saying “the story I’m telling myself is that you aren’t committed to this, and I’d like to check that with you” names your conclusion as a story, not a fact. It shows the other person where you are on the ladder and invites them to help you climb back down. Saying this out loud is a small way to embrace vulnerability: you are owning where you are, admitting you might be wrong, and asking for the other person’s help to see more clearly.

This connects to the “parts” we explored in Session 1. When a part of us feels threatened (the Worrier, the Perfectionist, the part that hates being let down), it tends to climb the ladder fast. Noticing that you are high on the ladder is often a sign that a part has grabbed the wheel.

The scenario

You are six months into the multi-organization collaborative from Session 1. The group is preparing a joint funding proposal, and at the last monthly meeting each organization agreed to draft its section and post it in the shared folder by Friday. On Friday afternoon, Jamie, the representative from one partner organization, posts this in the group channel:

“Our section will be late. We have other priorities right now.”

Nothing else. The full draft is due to the funder in two weeks, and you are coordinating the proposal.

On your own (3 min), notice your first reactions. Jot down:

  • What story do you find yourself telling about Jamie or their organization?
  • What feelings come up?
  • What would you be tempted to do next?

In the chat (7 min), let’s climb back down together:

  • What is the observable data? What did Jamie actually say and do?
  • What other stories could explain the same data? Come up with as many as you can.
  • What don’t you know? What information would help you test your story?
  • What could you ask Jamie to find out?

Tool Highlight: Questions for Climbing Down the Ladder

Use these on your own, before a conversation, or whenever you notice a strong reaction:

  • What did I actually see or hear? What would a video camera have recorded?
  • What story am I telling myself about it? What else could be true?
  • What part of me is reacting? What is it trying to protect?
  • What data might I be filtering out?
  • What am I assuming about their intentions?
  • What could I ask to test my story?

And in conversation:

  • “Here’s what I noticed… and the story I’m telling myself is… Can you help me understand what’s going on?”
  • “What am I missing?”
  • “How do you see it?”

Saying It Clearly: Nonviolent Communication

Once we have climbed down the ladder, we still need to say something. Nonviolent Communication (NVC), developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg, offers a simple structure for expressing ourselves honestly without blaming, and for hearing others without getting defensive. Rosenberg’s core insight is that behind every action, including the frustrating ones, is an attempt to meet a universal human need. When we connect at the level of needs, we can find more ways forward than when we argue about positions.

NVC has four components:

Component What it is Watch out for
Observation What happened, described specifically and without evaluation Mixing in judgment: “You’re always late,” “You ignored my email”
Feeling How I feel about it, in words that describe my own internal state “Faux feelings” that describe what someone did to me: ignored, disrespected, manipulated, abandoned
Need The universal human need behind the feeling: clarity, reliability, respect, inclusion, rest Confusing a need with a strategy: “I need you to reply within an hour” is a strategy; the need may be predictability
Request A specific, doable action I’d like, framed as something the other person can say yes or no to Demands, or vague requests like “be more responsible”

Naming What Happened: SBI + Intent

The observation is often the hardest part to get right, because our stories sneak into it. SBI + Intent gives it a shape:

Step What it is Example
Situation When and where “In Tuesday’s partner call…”
Behavior What a camera would see or hear. No labels, no motives. “…the timeline changed and our organization’s section moved up two weeks…”
Impact What it did to me, including how I felt “…and I felt caught off guard, because I’d already committed my staff’s time elsewhere.”
Intent Theirs, not mine. Ask, don’t assume. “Help me understand what was behind the change.”

Impact and intent are both true. A good intent doesn’t erase a hard impact, and a hard impact doesn’t prove a bad intent. Asking about intent is also a built-in way to climb down the ladder: you are checking your story instead of acting on it.

Asking for What You Need: The NVC Statement

Put together, NVC’s four components make a simple one-sentence form:

*“When ___ happens, I feel , because I need . Would you be willing to ___?“*

Notice that it says “because I need,” not “because you did.” In NVC, the other person’s action is what sparked the feeling, but the source of the feeling is your own need. That small shift keeps the statement about you rather than a verdict on them.

Two tips go with it:

  • State the emotion. No one can argue with how you felt. “I felt anxious” opens a conversation, while “you were careless” starts an argument.
  • Every ask gets a give. After you share what you need, invite the other person to share what they need: “What do you need from me?” It’s a trade, not a verdict. This is where common ground starts to show up.

You don’t need to use this structure word for word. In real conversations, it can sound stilted. The value is in the underlying moves: separating what happened from your interpretation, owning your feelings, naming the need, and making a clear request. For example:

Before: “You obviously don’t care about the proposal.”

After: “When I saw the message that your section would be late, without a new date, I felt anxious, because I need to be able to plan the full draft with the team and the funder deadline is close. Would you be willing to set up a quick call this week so we can figure out a timeline together?”

Needs Are Where Common Ground Lives

The most useful part of NVC for collaborative work may be the difference between needs and strategies. People rarely disagree about needs. Nearly everyone wants respect, fairness, reliability, being heard, and the chance to contribute. We disagree about strategies: the specific ways we try to meet those needs.

When a conversation feels stuck on positions (“We need the draft Friday” vs. “We can’t get to it until next month”), try asking what need each position is trying to meet. Often, there are several strategies that would meet both people’s needs, and finding them is far easier once the needs are on the table.

This is the same question we asked about working agreements in Session 1: What need does this serve, and whose need is it?

  • Part 2 Possible translations

In the chat (8 min), pick one or two of these statements and rewrite them as an observation, a feeling, a need, and a request. Use the Feelings and Needs Inventory if it helps.

  1. “You never read the materials before our meetings.”
  2. “I feel like you’re ignoring my input.”
  3. “This collaborative is completely disorganized.”
  4. “You can’t just make that decision without us.”

There’s no single right answer. Here are some possibilities:

  1. “In the last two meetings, a few questions came up that were answered in the pre-read (observation). I felt frustrated (feeling), because I want to use our meeting time well (need). Would you be willing to let me know if the pre-read isn’t arriving early enough for you to get to it (request)?”
  2. “When I shared my idea in the meeting and we moved on without discussing it (observation), I felt discouraged (feeling), because I want my contributions to be considered (need). Would you be willing to come back to it at our next meeting (request)?” Notice that “ignored” is a faux feeling: it describes what you think someone did, not how you feel.
  3. “We’ve rescheduled our last three meetings, and I wasn’t sure who was sending the agenda (observation). I feel uneasy (feeling) because I need some predictability to plan my time (need). Could we take ten minutes at the next meeting to agree on roles (request)?”
  4. “I learned about the change in the workplan from the newsletter (observation). I felt surprised and a bit hurt (feeling), because I value being included in decisions that affect my organization (need). Can we talk about how decisions like this will be made going forward (request)?”

Listening for Understanding

Speaking clearly is half of a courageous conversation. The other half, often the harder half, is listening. Most of us listen in order to reply: we are building our response while the other person is still talking. Listening for understanding means our goal, for now, is simply to understand what the other person is experiencing and what matters to them.

Listening for understanding is not agreeing. You can fully understand someone’s perspective and still disagree with it. But people are far more able to hear us once they feel heard.

Two of our core competencies live here. Active listening means staying fully present, asking open-ended questions, and building on others’ ideas while centering voices that are often unheard. To empathize is to listen deeply to others’ experiences, actively seek insight from their perspective, and connect with people from a place of love, respect, and curiosity.

A few practices help:

  • Get curious, not furious. Treat what you hear as information about the other person’s experience, not as an attack you need to defend against.
  • Reflect back what you heard, before you reply. Say it in your own words: “It sounds like the timeline has been really hard to manage alongside your other work. Did I get that right?” If they say no, try again until they say yes. It slows things down and means you are responding to what was actually said.
  • Listen for feelings and needs. In NVC terms, try to hear the feeling and need behind the words, especially behind blame or criticism. Someone who says “this process is a mess” may need clarity, or to feel that their time is respected.
  • Ask open-ended questions, not leading ones, and then actually listen to the answer. “What happened from your side?” invites the other person in. “Don’t you think that was a problem?” steers them toward your conclusion.
  • Embrace the silent space. After you ask a question or reflect something back, wait. People often share the most important thing after a pause.
  • Hold off on fixing. Advice, reassurance, and problem solving can all wait until the other person feels understood.
  • Notice your own ladder. If you find yourself getting defensive, that’s a cue that you have climbed up a rung or two. Pause, breathe, and come back to curiosity.

Tool Highlight: Curiosity Question Stems

  • “Help me understand…”
  • “What’s been going on for you with…?”
  • “What matters most to you about…?”
  • “What would a good outcome look like for you?”
  • “What’s getting in the way?”
  • “What am I missing?”
  • “Is there more?”

And for reflecting back:

  • “So what I’m hearing is… Did I get that right?”
  • “It sounds like you’re feeling ___ because ___ matters to you. Is that close?”
Warning Activity: Listening Pairs
  • Part 1 In pairs
  • Part 2 Whole-Class Discussion

In pairs (10 min total)

The speaker talks for 2-3 minutes about a work situation that is mildly frustrating right now. Keep it low-stakes: a process that isn’t working, a tool you’re fighting with, a schedule that’s too full.

The listener may only:

  • Reflect back what they hear, including feelings and needs they notice
  • Ask open-ended questions (not leading ones)
  • Let silence happen. If the speaker pauses, wait before jumping in.

The listener may not give advice, share their own similar story, or reassure.

Then take 2 minutes to debrief:

  • Speaker: What did it feel like to be listened to this way?
  • Listener: What was hard about not fixing or relating?

Switch roles and repeat. The person whose first name comes first alphabetically speaks first.

  • What did you notice as the speaker? As the listener?
  • What urges did you have to resist as the listener?

Practice: A Courageous Conversation

Now let’s bring all three tools together: noticing your own reactions, saying it clearly, and listening for understanding. We’ll start with Jamie’s scenario, then you’ll have a chance to prepare for a real conversation of your own.

Before the Conversation: Am I Ready?

A courageous conversation is more likely to go well when we go in prepared, not just with what we want to say, but with the right mindset. Brené Brown’s Engaged Feedback Checklist is a set of statements to check yourself against before giving feedback. Each one completes the sentence “I know that I’m ready to give feedback when…” Her central image is sitting next to the other person rather than across from them, with the problem in front of you both rather than between you. Her checklist also asks whether you are ready to listen and accept you may not fully understand, to own your part, to recognize the other person’s strengths, and to stay aware of power dynamics and bias.

If you can’t yet check most of the boxes, that’s useful information. It may mean you need more time on the ladder first. Or it may be a moment to embrace vulnerability and seek support: talking it through with a trusted colleague before the real conversation is a sign of strength, not weakness.

What It Sounds Like

Here is an example of what the full chain sounds like:

Speaker: “When the agenda changed an hour before the meeting, I didn’t have time to prepare the piece I was supposed to bring. I felt caught off guard, because I count on having time to get ready. What I need from you is a heads-up, even a quick one, before a change like that. And I’d like to understand what was going on for you.”

Listener (reflects first): “What I’m hearing is the timing made it hard to show up the way you wanted. Did I get that right?”

Listener (then shares their own needs): “That makes sense. From my side, when something comes up last minute, I feel pressure to get it on the agenda fast, because I don’t want to lose the window. What I need from you is some flexibility on short notice when it’s truly urgent.”

Name it concretely, reflect before responding, and trade asks rather than settling who’s right.

The scenario continues

You’ve climbed down the ladder, and you’ve asked Jamie for a short call. You want to understand what’s going on, share the impact of the delay, and figure out a path forward together.

In groups of 3 (20 minutes)

Each person takes one role. The person whose first name comes first alphabetically plays the coordinator. The next plays Jamie. The third is the observer.

  • Coordinator: Open the conversation with SBI + Intent and an NVC statement. Then listen for understanding, and ask Jamie what they need from you.
  • Jamie: Read the “Jamie’s role” tab (others, please don’t peek!). Respond as Jamie would.
  • Observer: Keep time and take notes. Listen for: observations vs. evaluations, feelings and needs, reflecting back, curious questions, and moments where either person climbs up the ladder.
  1. Prepare (3 min). The coordinator jots down an opening. Jamie reads their role. The observer reviews what to listen for.
  2. Conversation (7 min). The coordinator and Jamie talk. Aim for understanding and a next step, not a perfect resolution.
  3. Debrief (6 min). Jamie shares first: what helped you feel heard? What made you defensive? Then the coordinator, then the observer.
  4. Capture (4 min). As a group, note one or two things that worked well to share with the whole group.

For the person playing Jamie only.

You care about this collaborative and believe in the proposal. Two weeks ago, your executive director told you that your organization’s board hasn’t yet decided whether it can commit staff time to the work the proposal would fund. Until the board decides in three weeks, you’ve been asked not to put anything in writing that commits your organization. You were embarrassed and unsure how much you were allowed to say, so you posted a short message and hoped to sort it out later.

You also feel a bit sidelined: at the last meeting, your suggestion about the proposal’s community engagement approach was set aside quickly, and you’re not sure your organization’s perspective is valued.

Start out a little guarded. If the coordinator is curious and doesn’t blame you, gradually share more. If you feel judged, keep it short.

  • What did the coordinator do that helped Jamie open up?
  • Where did people climb up the ladder? What brought them back down?
  • What needs did you discover underneath the positions? Where was the common ground?

Tool Highlight: Conversation Prep

Use this to prepare for a real conversation. You can work through it on your own or with a trusted colleague.

1. Check the ladder

  • What actually happened? What would a camera have recorded?
  • What story am I telling myself? What else could be true?
  • What part of me is reacting, and what is it trying to protect?

2. Get clear on my side

  • What am I feeling?
  • What do I need? (Not the strategy, but the need underneath it.)
  • What is my purpose for this conversation? What would a good outcome look like for both of us?
  • What is my part in this situation?

3. Consider their side

  • What might they be feeling and needing?
  • What might they want me to understand?
  • What’s their thinking preference, and what will help them hear me?
  • What power dynamics are at play between us?
  • Whose voices or perspectives might be missing from how I’m seeing this?

4. Plan my opening

  • SBI + Intent: Situation, behavior, impact, and a curious question about their intent
  • NVC statement: “When ___ happens, I feel ___ , because I need ___ . Would you be willing to ___ ?”
  • The give: “What do you need from me?”

5. Check my readiness

  • Review the Engaged Feedback Checklist. Am I ready to sit next to them, with the problem in front of us?
  • When and where will I have this conversation? Is the channel right for what I need to say?
Warning Activity: Your Own Conversation
  • Part 1 Individual Preparation
  • Part 2 Sharing (optional)

On your own (8 min), bring to mind the conversation you thought about before the session, or another one you’d like to have. It can be big or small, current or past, work or personal. It doesn’t have to be dramatic, and it doesn’t need to involve anyone on this call. Choosing a smaller or less charged example than the first one that comes to mind is a perfectly good choice.

Work through the Conversation Prep prompts above. You don’t need to complete every prompt. Focus on the ones that feel most useful, and make sure you leave with your own NVC statement.

This is private. You won’t be asked to share the details of your situation.

In the chat or out loud, share only what you are comfortable sharing. Passing is always fine. If you share, keep it in the shape of an NVC statement (what you need) rather than a detailed account of what someone else did:

  • Which prompt was most useful?
  • What, if anything, shifted in how you see the situation?

Taking It Back

The point of this session is to equip you to have these conversations in your own work: with your internal teams, with partners or clients, and in the collaborative teams we facilitate. A few reminders:

  • Clear is kind. Avoiding a conversation usually isn’t kinder. It just postpones the hard part and lets it grow.
  • Check your ladder first. Before you speak, separate what happened from the story you’re telling about it.
  • Lead with curiosity. You probably don’t have the whole picture. Ask before you conclude.
  • Name needs, not just positions. Needs are where common ground lives.
  • Every ask gets a give. After you share what you need, ask what they need from you.
  • Listen until they feel heard. People can hear you more easily once they feel understood.
  • Mind the channel and the power. Hard conversations deserve a rich channel, and your position shapes how your words land.
  • It doesn’t have to be perfect. A clumsy, caring conversation is almost always better than none. Creative courage means embracing experimentation and the inevitable mistakes that come with trying something new. You can always come back and say, “I’d like to try that again.”
